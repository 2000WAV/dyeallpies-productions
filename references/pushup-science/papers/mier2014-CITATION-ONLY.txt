NUMBERS BELOW ARE SECONDHAND ONLY (via a WebSearch summary of Semantic Scholar/ResearchGate listing
pages, not the primary text itself -- treat with the same caution as any other un-verified secondary
citation in this archive, flagged accordingly wherever used):
  Study design (as reported by the citing summary): 37 men and women, 5 push-ups in the standard and
  modified position, plus 5-second isometric holds in the up and down positions of each; vertical
  GRF via force platform, expressed as %BW.
  Maximal relative load (standard push-up): men 97.7 +/- 8.1% BW; women 80.0 +/- 3.9% BW.
  Maximal relative load (modified/knee push-up): men 79.7 +/- 7.4% BW; women 68.2 +/- 3.0% BW.
  %ROM (full range of motion): standard men 67.7 +/- 6.1%, women 50.1 +/- 11.4%; modified men
  66.6 +/- 6.9%, women 60.1 +/- 8.9%.
  Conclusion (as summarized): women perform the push-up with less relative load and less range of
  motion than men, a difference the authors attribute to movement-pattern differences that may
  change with fatigue.

CAUTION ON THE ~97.7% FIGURE: this is notably higher than every other %BW push-up figure in this
archive (Gouvali 2005: 66.4%; Eckel 2017 men: 72-77%; Ebben 2011 via secondary citation: 64%) --
possibly because it reflects a MAXIMAL/near-full dynamic load during active pushing rather than a
static isometric hold, or a different definition of "% body weight supported" (e.g. peak vs mean,
or including the acceleration/impact component of an active push-up rather than a quasi-static
value). Do not average this number in with the other studies' figures without first confirming the
methodological difference from the primary text -- flag this as an open question for a future
session with access to the full PDF (try: an institutional VPN/library proxy, a different search
engine cache of the WKU digitalcommons PDF, or emailing the corresponding author).

Also cited (secondhand, via Eckel et al. 2017's Discussion, itself in eckel2017-...-extract.txt in
