## 1. Hooks in the first 1–3 seconds

- **Skip rate is now a first-party, in-app Reels metric** (confirmed in the pull-up session,
  §6 of the prior file: Instagram shipped a Retention chart + Skip Rate metric in Reels
  Insights, reported ~August 2025). 2026 benchmark blogs converge on **skip rate under ~20%
  in the first 3 s = healthy, 20–35% = typical, over ~40% = weak hook** — repeated across
  independent creator-analytics blogs (metricool.com, babbleboxx.com, truefuturemedia.com,
  retensis.com, volanea.com, ytviews.in) with consistent-enough numbers to be more than one
  blog echoing another, but still **no named Instagram dataset behind the exact thresholds
  (blog consensus)**.
- **The "1.7-second scroll decision" figure reappeared this session**, attributed loosely to
  "Meta research," same as the pull-up session's single-source sighting — still **unverified,
  not independently confirmed against a primary Meta source**, carried forward as the same
  caveat rather than upgraded.
- **Skip rate specifically measures exits in the first 3 seconds** (not the whole video) —
  consistent across every source that defines it; this matches what the pull-up notes already
  established and is not new information, only reconfirmed.
- **Cold-open on the payoff vs. text-hook vs. title card**: general short-form-editing sources
  (socialync.io, swngproductions.com, pixelplot.ai, chaplinai.pro) describe "story cold-open"
  (drop into the result/tension before any setup) as one of a handful of reliable hook
  archetypes, and repeat that **"a strong hook without a payoff is worse than a weak one"** —
  i.e. a cold-open on a dramatic verdict only works if the video actually delivers that
  verdict, or watch-time collapses when viewers feel baited. This is **opinion / editorial
  consensus, not a measured study**, but it is a real constraint on your existing pull-up
  formula (title card promising "33-point pose tracking, 3D angles, EMG heat map" and then
  delivering exactly that).
- **Face-to-camera vs. not, and motion-in-frame**: no fitness-specific or pose-estimation-
  specific data was found this session on face-to-camera hooks. General advice repeats that
  *any* motion in the opening frame (a rep starting, a number counting up, an overlay
  animating in) outperforms a static frame, because the platform's own thumbnail/scroll
  behavior rewards perceived motion — **opinion, consistent with why your title card already
  opens on Dennis standing at the bar rather than a static graphic, but not measured data**.
- **Nothing new found specific to "AI analyzes my form" hooks this session** beyond what the
  pull-up notes already covered (OnForm, Ochy, Kinovea, the skeleton-overlay TikTok trend
  lead). No additional named account or video was found and independently verified.
- **Most viewing is muted** (70–80%+, carried over from the pull-up notes, not re-verified
  this session but not contradicted either) — still the reason on-screen text has to carry
  the hook. Directly relevant to a push-up video shot head-on from the floor: the head coming
  toward the lens at the bottom of each rep is itself a strong, unmissable motion cue that
  works muted, independent of any text.

## 2. Visual salience and colour: two-colour heat map vs. multi-hue rainbow

This is the one question this session had strong peer-reviewed grounding for.

- **Crameri, Shephard & Heron (2020), "The misuse of colour in science communication,"
  *Nature Communications* 11:5444** (peer-reviewed, **open access, CC BY 4.0**, full PDF
  saved to `virality/crameri2020.pdf`): argues rainbow-like colour maps (a) are not
  perceptually uniform — equal steps in the data do not look like equal steps in colour, so
  the map can visually manufacture false boundaries/bands that are not in the underlying
  data, and (b) are frequently unreadable to colour-vision-deficient viewers. The paper's
  own recommendation is *not* "avoid all bright colour" — it is to use perceptually uniform,
  ordered colour maps and to reserve strong colour contrast for genuine, meaningful
  transitions in the data, not decorative variety. **Peer-reviewed, primary source directly
  read.**
- **Borland & Taylor (2007), "Rainbow Color Map (Still) Considered Harmful,"
  *IEEE CG&A* 27(2)** (peer-reviewed, **not open access** — paywalled IEEE article, no legal
  full-text copy found this session, see `virality/SOURCES.md`): the foundational paper in
  this literature, cited secondhand via abstracts/summaries only. Its argument (repeated
  consistently across every secondary source found): the rainbow map has no perceptual
  ordering (viewers can't tell "more red" from "more purple" ranks without the legend),
  uncontrolled luminance obscures fine detail, and it introduces visual artifacts —
  apparent edges/bands — that aren't in the data. **Cite by citation only, not directly
  read this session.**
- **Rogowitz & Treinish** were searched for by name; no specific paper text was retrieved
  this session (their "Why Should Engineers and Scientists Be Worried About Color?" /
  related IBM-era work is the other commonly-cited foundational source in this literature per
  secondary mentions, but it was not independently fetched — **not verified this session,
  omit rather than cite as read**).
- **"What's wrong with the rainbow?" (ISPRS J. Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing 194, 2022)**:
  publisher page states the license is CC BY-NC-ND (i.e. legitimately open access), and a
  search-snippet-level summary describes a systematic review of **37,000+ figures across
  11,808 papers** (infovis, neuroscience, hydrology, geography, remote sensing, planetary
  science) finding rainbow use is declining in visualization-specific venues but persists
  elsewhere, and that **rainbow maps are used "because people like them, they attract
  attention, and the hues are highly discriminable and nameable, thus memorable"** — i.e.
  the review's own framing is that rainbow maps have a real *attention/memorability*
  advantage even where they have a real *accuracy* cost. The direct PDF fetch failed (see
  SOURCES.md) so this is from the search snippet, not the full text — **peer-reviewed
  source, but only secondhand-summarized this session.**
- **Ware et al., "Rainbow Colormaps Are Not All Bad" (IEEE CG&A, 2023)**: a more recent,
  partial rebuttal in the same literature — participants were faster and more accurate with
  perceptually uniform maps than rainbow maps in diagnostic/reading tasks, but the paper's
  title itself signals rainbow maps aren't universally disqualified, e.g. for tasks that need
  fast categorical discrimination rather than precise quantitative reading. **Peer-reviewed,
  secondhand summary only, not fetched in full.**
- **Google's Turbo colormap (Research blog, 2019)**: not academic-peer-reviewed but a
  primary engineering source (Google Research) explaining *why* a high-contrast rainbow-style
  map (Jet) persists despite the perceptual critique — Jet/Turbo-style maps are kept because
  they give strong local contrast and "pop" for quick visual triage, and Turbo was
  specifically designed to keep that high-contrast pop while fixing Jet's false-detail/
  banding/colour-blindness problems. This is the closest thing found to an authoritative
  statement that **high local contrast and perceptual accuracy are two different design
  goals that can partially, not fully, be reconciled** — directly relevant to "science vs.
  viral."
- **Preattentive processing / colour pop-out (Treisman & Gelade 1980, Feature Integration
  Theory; summarized via eagereyes.org and Springer/PMC secondary sources, not the original
  paper itself)**: a target that differs from its surroundings by a single salient feature
  (e.g. one saturated hue against a different hue) is detected in parallel, pre-attentively,
  regardless of how many distractors surround it — "pops out." A target requiring a
  *conjunction* of features (e.g. this exact shade *and* this exact position on a 6-hue
  ramp) requires slower, serial search. **This is the direct mechanistic argument for why a
  two-colour blue-to-red map reads faster than a 6-hue rainbow ramp**: blue vs. red is a
  single, unambiguous, pre-attentive colour-opposition judgment ("cold" vs. "hot" almost
  instantly), while placing a value on a blue-cyan-green-yellow-orange-red ramp requires the
  viewer to consciously rank a colour against a remembered order — a serial, attention-
  demanding task, exactly wrong for a video most people are half-watching while scrolling.
  **Peer-reviewed theory, well-established, but applied here as an inference/analogy — no
  study found this session that tested colour-map hue-count directly against short-form
  video attention/retention.**
- **"Opposing colour pairs enhance contrast"** (ACM SAP 2025, "Reading Between the Colors,"
  search-snippet level only): supports that a blue/red (opponent-colour) pair is read faster
  for quick comparisons than a full hue ramp, because the visual system prioritizes hue
  *difference*, not absolute hue identification, for fast judgments. Consistent with the
  Treisman argument above. **Peer-reviewed venue, secondhand summary only.**
- **Thermal-camera / "heat vision" aesthetic in fitness content**: real, active engineering
  literature exists (not the same as "went viral," but establishes the aesthetic has a
  genuine scientific analog, which matters for "honest colour" framing) —
  **Viafora, L.A. et al., "An infrared imaging technique for weightlifting exercise
  assessment," *Applied Optics* 63(28), 7529 (2024)**, covered in an Optica.org news release
  (2024-09) and PMC8272228, **"Detection of Muscle Activation during Resistance Training
  Using Infrared Thermal Imaging"** (peer-reviewed, PMC-indexed): both describe real infrared
  thermography detecting skin-surface heat correlated with muscle exertion during resistance
  training — a legitimate, separate physiological signal from EMG, but visually similar
  (a body with warmer/cooler regions highlighted). **This means "blue = cold/resting, red =
  hot/working" is not just a viral gimmick — it mirrors a real, separately-measurable
  physiological phenomenon (skin thermography), even though your heat map itself is driven
  by inverse-dynamics/EMG-style muscle activation + fatigue (per
  `references/pullup-science` muscle-heat-map-physics work), not actual skin temperature.**
  Worth a one-line on-screen or caption disclosure that the colour represents *activation +
  fatigue*, not literal heat, to stay honest while keeping the thermal-camera visual
  language — no source found this session that specifically studied whether disclosing this
  distinction helps or hurts a video's performance; that gap is **not filled, flag as
  unknown rather than guessing.**
- **No specific named viral "thermal camera at the gym" or "EMG heat map" video was found and
  independently verified this session** — searches returned general TikTok discovery pages
  (#gymcam, #thermal) rather than a specific traceable viral post, and the Optica/PMC
  material above is science reporting, not creator content. **This is a gap, consistent with
  the same gap noted in the pull-up session for the muscle-heat-map format generally — still
  no confirmed direct precedent, for or against.**

**Net for Q2**: there is real, peer-reviewed tension, not a clean answer. The literature says
rainbow/multi-hue maps are worse for *accurate quantitative reading* (Crameri 2020, Borland
2007, Ware 2023) but is explicitly acknowledged (by the rainbow-critique literature itself,
via the ISPRS review) to be *better at grabbing and holding attention* and more memorable —
which is precisely why it persists despite 19+ years of "considered harmful" papers. A
two-colour blue-red opposition additionally has a specific perceptual-speed advantage
(preattentive pop-out, Treisman) that a 6-hue ramp does not. This directly supports Dennis's
belief that the pure blue-red set #1 outperformed the anatomy-rainbow set #3 — not proof
(no study tested this exact comparison), but a coherent, sourced mechanism.

## 4. Structure for a 45–60 s "science + viral" reel

- **Length bracket**: carried over unchanged from the pull-up session's Socialinsider 6M-Reel
  dataset (§1 of the prior file) — 45–60 s remains the top engagement/median-views bracket in
  that brand-account sample; not re-verified this session, no new push-up-specific length
  data found.
- **Pattern interrupts**: multiple blog sources (edicionvideopro.com, joyspace.ai,
  inceptly.com, increditors.com) converge on **a pattern interrupt (text overlay, cut, camera
  change, B-roll) every 3–5 seconds for 15–60 s content**, with claimed retention deltas
  (e.g. "58% vs. 41%" for interrupt-every-4-seconds vs. static talking-head) that **could not
  be traced to a named study — treat the mechanism (frequent visual change sustains
  attention) as directionally credible and widely repeated, but the specific percentages as
  unverified marketing-copy precision, same caution as the pull-up session's "72–84% vs.
  65–78%" hook-type numbers.** For a push-up set, each rep itself is a natural pattern-
  interrupt unit (the head-toward-camera motion, a rep-count tick, a verdict flash) — the
  format may already satisfy this by construction rather than needing added B-roll.
- **Does explaining the science help or hurt?** The clearest concrete claim found: **"47% of
  viewers lose attention in explainer videos within the first 10 seconds if no hook is
  present"** (gitnux.org, webtonic.io — **blog consensus, no named study**) — read together
  with **"educational Reels average 3.92% engagement rate, described as the highest-
  engagement content category on the platform"** (same tier of sources). The throughline
  across every source on this question: **educational framing does not inherently hurt
  retention, but only if the hook still lands in the first seconds** — the science content
  is not competing with entertainment content on completion rate, it's competing with itself
  on whether the opening 1–3 s justifies staying. This is consistent with, and extends, the
  pull-up session's §2 note that "cold-open on the payoff" is standard practice — for this
  video specifically, it argues for opening on a number or verdict (e.g. "84 is the world
  record. Here's what his rep #1 looked like against it") rather than opening on unexplained
  setup.
- **PEEK dataset (arXiv:2109.03154, "A Large Dataset of Learner Engagement with Educational
  Videos")**: a real, large-scale academic dataset exists specifically on this question, but
  the full text could not be extracted this session (WebFetch returned corrupted/binary
  content on the PDF — see `virality/SOURCES.md`). **Only the existence and topic of this
  dataset is confirmed, not its findings — do not cite any specific finding from it, it is
  listed here as a lead for a future session with better PDF access, not as evidence.**
- **On-screen text density and caption first line**: no push-up- or fitness-specific data
  found this session; nothing found that contradicts or adds to the pull-up session's §5
  notes (cover-frame text 3–5 words/48–60pt, caption first line as its own hook by general
  short-form consensus, not fitness-specific data).

## 5. Looping and the ending

- **Mechanism, consistently repeated across sources (slidycreator.com and others,
  **blog consensus, but the underlying logic is platform-plausible and not new this
  session**)**: a seamless loop lets the video restart before the viewer consciously decides
  to rewatch, which can push measured watch-time/completion above 100% of the clip's raw
  length; both major platforms are described as weighting completion/rewatch behavior
  heavily in distribution. This is the same claim, same confidence level, as the pull-up
  session's §2 — **not re-verified against a primary source this session, carried forward
  unchanged.**
  workflow **not enhanced this session — no push-up-specific loop-length study found.**
- **What to put in the last 2 seconds**: general advice converges on ending the frame on
  something that visually rhymes with the opening frame (same pose, same framing, same
  on-screen graphic state) so the cut from last-frame-to-first-frame doesn't register as a
  hard edit. For a push-up set shot head-on with the head coming toward the lens each rep,
  the natural loop point is **the bottom of a rep** (head closest to camera) cutting back to
  **the bottom of the first rep** — both are already the same pose/framing by construction,
  which is a stronger natural loop anchor than the pull-up format has (top-of-bar vs.
  dead-hang are visually more different than rep-bottom vs. rep-bottom on a push-up).
  **This is an inference from the general loop-editing advice applied to your specific
  camera angle, not a sourced push-up-specific claim.**

## Concrete recommendations for the push-up reel

1. **Open on a verdict or number, not a setup.** Cold-open on the single most striking
   comparison (e.g. rep count vs. the 24-hour record of 46,001, or "USMC standard says X —
   here's rep #1") as on-screen text in the first 1–3 s; every source on hooks and on
   educational-content retention agrees the opening seconds carry the whole video (§1, §4).
2. **Use the two-colour blue-to-red heat map, not the anatomy-rainbow scale, for the primary
   cut** — the preattentive pop-out mechanism (Treisman) and the "opposing colour pairs
   enhance contrast" finding (§2) give a real, sourced reason the pure blue-red look reads
   faster on a scrolling, often-muted feed than a 6-hue ramp, consistent with Dennis's
   existing belief from set #1 vs. set #3 performance. This is inference from mechanism, not
   a direct study of this exact comparison — say so if asked, don't oversell it as proven.
3. **Keep the colour scientifically honest in what it's computed from** (activation +
   fatigue per the existing muscle-heat-map-physics formulation), but disclose in one on-
   screen or caption line that it is not literal skin temperature — real infrared
   thermography of muscle exertion exists as a separate, legitimate technique (Viafora 2024,
   PMC8272228, §2), so the "thermal camera" visual language is defensible, but only if you
   don't imply the video *is* thermal imaging when it's derived from pose/force estimation.
4. **If a scientifically-truer multi-hue map matters for credibility, keep it as a secondary
   overlay or a "science mode" cutaway shot inside the same video** (2–3 s window) rather
   than the whole video's primary look — this reconciles the Crameri/Borland accuracy
   critique with the attention/memorability case the rainbow-critique literature itself
   concedes rainbow maps have (§2), without giving up either.
5. **Target the 45–60 s bracket** for the primary cut (carried over from the pull-up
   session's Socialinsider data, unchanged this session, §4) — your 117 s source will need a
   real cut, not just a trim.
6. **Build in a pattern interrupt roughly every 3–5 s** — for this format that likely means:
   verdict flash, rep-count tick, colour shift on the heat map, and the camera's own head-
   toward-lens motion each rep, which may already satisfy this by construction (§4).
7. **End the cut on the bottom of a rep (head closest to camera) and open on the same framing**
   — this format's camera angle gives a more natural loop point than the pull-up format had,
   since rep-bottom-to-rep-bottom is already visually near-identical (§5).
8. **Lean into the chest-to-floor / hand-release objectivity angle** in on-screen text or
   caption, deliberately, the way the pull-up video leaned into chin-over-bar strictness —
   this is a real, recurring, sourced fitness-community debate (elbow flare, hip sag, partial
   depth, §3) and is comment-bait in a good way: it gives people something specific and
   defensible to argue about.
9. **Put the 24-hour record (46,001, Charles Servizio, 1993, unbroken 30+ years) somewhere
   in the video or caption** — it is the single most quotable, verified, surprising number
   found this session (§3) and needs no interpretation to land.
10. **Do not claim a specific named "went viral" push-up precedent with numbers** — the
    Babilya example (§3) is real but only partially sourced (no date, no view count), and no
    other specific viral push-up video was confirmed this session. If you want a "this format
    has worked before" line, use the Guinness records and the general chest-to-floor debate,
    not an invented or half-sourced "viral video" claim.
11. **Keep hashtags at exactly 5, one line**, per the existing CLAUDE.md/pull-up-session rule
    (platform-enforced cap since December 2025) — unchanged, not re-researched this session.
12. **Pull the in-app Retention chart and Skip Rate for the existing 140k-view pull-up video
    before finalizing the push-up cut** — same recommendation as the pull-up session's #10,
    repeated because it is still the best available data source and still hasn't been
    reported as done.
