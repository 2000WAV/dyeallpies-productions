Rolling back in time

Karl St-Arnaud
6 min read
·
Oct 17, 2024

--

This picture shows an iPad lying on the floor with a mirror on top of it reflecting the iPad display. The clock reflected in the mirror shows 3 minutes 18 seconds and 983 milliseconds, offset by 17 milliseconds compared to what is displayed on the iPad: 3 minutes 19 seconds and 000 milliseconds. How was this image produced? The answer to the questions is in the first sentence of the results section and explained visually in Figure 3, but before you jump there, think about it.

Context

About 30 years ago, film cameras were used to take pictures. When the user pressed the button, a shutter inside the camera opened for a fraction of a second, letting the light pass and exposing the film until the shutter closed again. The light created a chemical reaction with the film, imprinting every light reaching the film during the shutter’s opening definitively on the pelicule. In context, if a sports car traveled 30m between the opening and closing of the shutter, the film would have shown a blurry car traveling a distance of 30m.

Then came the digital camera, which could imprint the scene content in a digital version of those pellicles. Instead of a chemical reaction, a semiconductor accumulates electric charge as the light reaches the pixel. The first version of digital film to be integrated into cameras was a CCD (Charge-coupled device). As with the film, with the CCD, the complete sensor is exposed simultaneously to light until the sensor enters the reading stage. As depicted in Figure 2, during that stage, every pixel’s charge is transferred to the unique amplifier and analog-to-digital converter, which essentially transforms the electric signal of every pixel into digital information you save on a memory disk. Since every pixel needed to be read sequentially, this process was quite long, resulting in a very slow frame rate if you wanted to capture video, for example.

CMOS (Complementary metal oxide semiconductor) came as an alternative to CCD with a faster readout time. This is partly achieved by reading the charge value in every pixel in several sequences interleaved with exposure sequences. As shown in Figure 2, the charge of every row is transferred in a reading bus after the exposure stage. During the reading process of that row (referred to as the readout process), the sensor continues to expose the next row. To achieve this, each pixel is equipped with electric circuitry to move the charge out of the pixel and execute readout externally, as shown by the dark square in the corner of each pixel. Instead of exposing the sensor all at once and reading every pixel afterward, which was quite long, the sensor exposes and reads each row individually. Since the exposure isn’t happening simultaneously for each row, a fast-moving car will appear to stretch from top to bottom. CMOS and CCD exposure aren’t instantaneous, so both will show a blurry moving object.

The readout process described for CCD and CMOS is often called global and rolling shutter. The camera can read one row at a time in rolling shutter mode. Therefore, the timing between the start of exposure of two rows equals the readout time of one row. The readout of one row is generally in the range of microseconds, but when aggregated on a full sensor, the full readout time of one frame can reach a few milliseconds.

Results

The main photo in the article shows an example of the rolling shutter effect. An iPad Air four was installed on the floor, playing a Chrono video with a frame rate of 60Hz, meaning each image is displayed for ~16 milliseconds (ms). On top of the iPad, a mirror reflects the display. The pixel 4a 5G camera is placed such that the top and bottom rows of the sensor, respectively, image the mirror and the iPad. In the image captured by the camera, one can see that the top of the sensor was captured later compared to the bottom part of the sensor at approximately one screen frame. The scene was taken to have some bright objects in the background to get the exposure time as low as possible and minimize blur on the number displayed.

A second set of captures was taken to validate that a rolling shutter created the timing artifact. The same settings were used for the camera, and a laptop was used as a display with a refresh rate of 60Hz. One capture was taken the same way as the original image, and the other was taken with the camera flipped upside down. On both captures, the timing difference is present, but when the camera is flipped, the time offset is now negative, showing the readout orientation of the sensor. On this second set of captures, the light wasn’t that bright outside, leading to a slightly higher exposure time and a blurrier display.

Discussion

The experimentation presented in this article is a quick demonstration of the rolling shutter effect. In a more controlled and scientific context, there is a more accurate way to measure the impact of a rolling shutter. For example, in the CNET example of the iPhone 15, they use a flickering video displaying a bright and dark pattern to measure the read-out time accurately [1]. Another method DXOmark proposed [2] uses a panel such as the IQL camera timing test chart to measure the time it takes for an LED to travel from left to right on a display. In the end, keep in mind that the camera itself applies several post-processing setups to correct most of the artifacts created by the sensor.

The experiment was replicated with a more recent phone, the Pixel 8. It wasn’t possible to reproduce this timing artifact image. The camera is one of the phone features that improve the most every year, and reducing that readout will, of course, create a better picture. For comparison, in the CNET review, the latest iPhone 15 pro showed a rolling shutter of ~5ms [1]. Most smartphone cameras use a stabilization algorithm to correct the rolling shutter effect. For example, if you search “rolling shutter,” most images will show the impact of a rolling shutter with a rotating blade. However, these days, chances are that you won’t be able to capture this artifact as drastically with your phone as they are correcting for it. When testing it with three different phones, it was found that newer phones indeed show lower rolling shutter artifacts, as shown in Figure 5. The pixel 4a 5G still produces the type of rolling shutter common artifact (the blades are bent to the left). Pixel 8 applied severe correction as the experiment showed the display refresh rate syncing with the rotating blade, as shown in Figure 6.
