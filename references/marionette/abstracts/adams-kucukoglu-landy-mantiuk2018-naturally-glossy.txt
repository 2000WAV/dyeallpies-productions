SUMMARY: glossiness (how "shiny" vs. "matte" a surface reads) is judged
mainly from the pattern of specular highlights -- their brightness and
sharpness -- relative to the diffuse body of the object; sharper, brighter
highlights read as glossier. Perceived gloss depends on the STATISTICS of
the scene's illumination (natural illumination, with its skewed distribution
of a few very bright light sources against a darker average, supports
correct gloss/solidity judgments); removing that skew (e.g. by tone-mapping
or by reversing the overhead-light direction) makes the same object look
LESS glossy/less like a real solid lit surface, and this effect is largest
when the object is viewed in isolation (without a full background scene
providing context) -- close to this project's situation (isolated puppet on
plain wall, no complex scene).

RELEVANCE to item 9: reinforces the shape-from-shading/specular-highlight
argument (see Kleffner & Ramachandran 1992, Fleming Dror & Adelson 2003):
solidity reads from a physically-plausible, spatially concentrated, single-
direction highlight/shading pattern, not from uniform brightness -- directly
against an "emit light from every surface equally" neon-glow look, and in
favour of directional shading + a small sharp specular highlight + an
occluding-contour-consistent silhouette.
