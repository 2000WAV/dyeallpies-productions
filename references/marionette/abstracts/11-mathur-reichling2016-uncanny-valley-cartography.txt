SUMMARY (from the abstract): the first study to demonstrate a genuine
Uncanny Valley effect using a large, OBJECTIVELY CHOSEN sample of 80
real-world robot faces (not just artificially blended/morphed images, which
prior studies relied on and which produced inconsistent results), rating
their likability across the human-likeness spectrum. Also ran an "investment
game" showing the uncanny-valley dip affects not just explicit likability
ratings but implicit judgments of a robot's social TRUSTWORTHINESS, tied to
the same facial-expression cues used to judge humans.

KEY NUMBERS: NOT FOUND -- the paper's quantitative valley curve (likability
vs. human-likeness, the location/depth of the dip) is in the paywalled full
text and figures, not recoverable from the abstract alone.

RELEVANCE: confirms the uncanny valley is a real, measurable effect (not
just an anecdotal/inconsistent one), using real-world robot faces rather
than only synthetic morphs -- relevant caution for section 1/7: since the
marionette is stylized and not attempting photoreal human skin/face detail,
it should sit safely on the near (low-human-likeness) side of the valley
described here, and pushing individual features (e.g. a very detailed face)
toward higher realism while the rest of the body stays stylized would risk
crossing INTO the valley rather than out of it (see also Chattopadhyay &
MacDorman 2016, inconsistent-realism finding, below).
