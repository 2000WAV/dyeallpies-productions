retrieved 2026-09-12), citing Van Hurkman 2013 ("Color Correction Handbook") and
Malkiewicz & Mullen 2009 ("Cinematography") as its own sources for the numeric claims.

Classic (film-era) recipe: underexpose by about TWO f-stops (cited to Van Hurkman
2013, p.31), often using a neutral-density filter so the aperture itself doesn't need
to change; use 3200K tungsten-balanced stock/white-balance instead of 5000K
daylight-balance so that practical/artificial lights in frame render white while
unlit/ambient areas render "moonlight blue"; add a blue tint and increase contrast in
grading; shadow areas are deliberately kept slightly under-lit relative to a real night
exposure, to preserve the higher-contrast look associated with night vision.

Modern digital exception, explicitly named: Mad Max: Fury Road (2015) shot day-for-
night scenes deliberately OVER-exposed (opposite of the classic recipe), exploiting
digital sensor dynamic range so highlight/shadow detail survives; the shots were then
pulled down in exposure and given a bluish grade in post, avoiding the shadow clipping
that classic underexposure risks. (This matches, independently, the "John Seale /
~2 stops over on the Alexa" account found via WebSearch of trade-press secondary
sources -- not independently re-verified against a primary ASC/DP interview in this
archive, so only the Wikipedia-sourced version of the claim is treated as citable.)

Digital-era additions: intensifying glare/bloom around practical light sources (window
glow, headlights, streetlights) that would otherwise be less visible in daylight is
now common practice to sell the "night" read -- directly relevant to item 12's own
compositing recipe (the emissive figure's bloom is exactly this kind of "practical
light source glow" cue, on a shot that is not lit like night at all outside the
figure itself).
