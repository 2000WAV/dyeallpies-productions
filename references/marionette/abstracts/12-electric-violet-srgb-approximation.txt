Spectral violet is 380-435 nm at the short-wavelength end of the visible spectrum
(Violet (color) infobox); "Shades of violet" narrows this further for its own naming
scheme, treating 380-420 nm as "violet proper" and 420-450 nm as a separate "indigo"
band. Both pages state plainly that an sRGB/RGB display CANNOT reproduce spectral
violet light directly: violet wavelengths lie outside the sRGB gamut (the spectral
locus at short wavelengths is outside the sRGB triangle), so displays fake it by mixing
blue light at high intensity with red light at lower intensity.

Three named sRGB approximations, in increasing "redness" (all HSV V=100%, S=100%):
- "Violet" (Wikipedia's own infobox colour for the article, sourced to the W3C CSS
  Color Module Level 3 extended colour keywords): hex #8000FF, sRGB (128,0,255), HSV
  (270 deg, 100%, 100%). Called "colour wheel violet" in Shades of violet, an
  approximation of ~417 nm, actually closer to what that page calls "indigo" territory.
- "Electric violet" (Shades of violet, sourced to an archived third-party "HTML Color
  Chart" page -- Wikipedia itself flags this source with a "failed verification" tag,
  so treat the hex as a widely-used convention rather than an authoritative standard):
  hex #8F00FF, sRGB (143,0,255), HSV (274 deg, 100%, 100%), ISCC-NBS name "Vivid
  violet". The article states this is "the closest approximation to middle spectrum
  violet that can be made on a computer screen, given the limitations of the sRGB
  colour gamut" and pins it to ~400 nm, the MIDDLE of the 380-420 nm violet band -- this
  is the number requested for this task ("electromagnetic violet" ~400-420 nm).
- "Vivid violet" / "extreme violet" (same source/caveat): hex #9F00FF, sRGB
  (159,0,255), approximating the extreme short-wavelength edge (~380 nm).

Two important non-violet lookalikes to avoid: the WEB COLOR literally named "violet"
(#EE82EE, X11/CSS keyword) is actually a pale tint of MAGENTA (equal red and blue plus
some green), not a spectral-violet approximation at all -- the article calls this out
explicitly. "Dark violet" / X11 (#9400D3, RGB 148,0,211) is the closest common named
colour to a desaturated/pigment version of electric violet (~11% less blue channel,
substantially less luminous) and is offered as the "artist's pigment" analogue rather
than the "electric"/emissive one.

Mechanism note (Violet (color), "Optics" section, unsourced/cite-needed tag on the
underlying claim but consistent with standard opponent-process colour theory): violet
appears slightly reddish compared to spectral blue, despite being at the OPPOSITE end
of the spectrum from red, because the S-cone (short-wavelength) contributes a small
amount of "red" signal to the red-vs-green opponent channel; and the S-cones
contribute very little to the luminance (lightness) channel at all, which is why
"the colour violet appears dark" -- a direct, citable mechanism for why violet is the
LOWEST-luminance of the saturated primaries at matched saturation (see
data/12-neon-black-numbers.csv for the computed L* values of all three violet
approximations against pure red and other saturated hues, using the same sRGB-to-L*
formulas already archived under item 9, abstracts/wikipedia-cielab-srgb-formulas.txt).
