subtitles: Evidence from eye movements. PLOS ONE 13(6): e0199331.
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0199331 (open access, CC BY).

SUMMARY: Eye-tracking study, 74 English/Polish/Spanish viewers watching clips
subtitled at 12, 16 and 20 characters per second (cps) — i.e. testing well above
the traditional "six-second rule" (~12 cps). Viewers coped well even at 20 cps:
most could read the subtitles fully AND still follow the on-screen action: no
loss of image-scene recognition at the fastest speed tested. Slower subtitles
(12 cps) triggered MORE re-reading and were rated as more boring/frustrating in
the English-soundtrack condition, i.e. slow is not automatically safer. Faster,
unreduced-text subtitles were preferred for the English clips; slower,
edited-down text was preferred for the Hungarian (unknown-language) clips.

RELEVANCE: the caption's URL (github.com/DyeAllPies/dyeallpies-productions) is
read once, at reel size, with no dialogue-comprehension task competing for
attention, which is an easier case than film subtitling. This paper is the
counter-evidence to the "give it lots of hold time or nobody reads it" instinct:
a modern viewer accustomed to fast subtitles can read short strings well above
BBC's 160-180 wpm broadcast-caption baseline (Section 4 of
brand/bbc-accessibility-subtitles-guide.html) without extra hold time — but the
one-time, unrepeatable exposure of a 1-second sting (no rewind, no second
subtitle event a moment later) is the opposite of the repeated-exposure film
context this paper measures, so its cps numbers are a floor for attention, not
a substitute for the sting's own on-screen hold-time budget.
