Brachiation-based movement as a theoretical framework for addressing 
technology-related postural dysfunction: An evolutionary and neuromuscular 
perspective.

Ishibashi RF(1).

Author information:
(1)Elanare Institute, Tokyo, Japan; Japan 2E & Neurodivergent Initiative, Tokyo, 
Japan; Japanese Association for Gifted & Twice Exceptional Children, Tokyo, 
Japan; Faculty of Liberal Arts, The Open University of Japan, Chiba, Japan. 
Electronic address: rysh.cact@gmail.com.

BACKGROUND: Technology-related postural dysfunction, characterized by forward 
head posture, thoracic hyperkyphosis, and scapular dyskinesis, represents a 
growing public health concern. Upper Crossed Syndrome (UCS)-a pattern of muscle 
imbalance involving overactive upper trapezius and inhibited lower trapezius, 
serratus anterior, and deep cervical flexors-is prevalent among desk workers. 
While rehabilitation protocols targeting individual muscles exist, an integrated 
framework addressing the evolutionary origins of this dysfunction remains 
underdeveloped.
OBJECTIVE: This theoretical paper proposes that brachiation-based 
movements-particularly unilateral hanging with oscillatory perturbation-may 
offer a biomechanically optimal approach for addressing UCS and related postural 
dysfunctions by restoring muscle activation patterns consistent with our 
evolutionary heritage.
METHODS: A comprehensive review of electromyographic (EMG) literature on 
trapezius, serratus anterior, rotator cuff, and deep cervical flexor activation 
during various exercises was synthesized with evolutionary biomechanics research 
on human brachiation capacity.
RESULTS: EMG evidence demonstrates that overhead movements and pull-up exercises 
preferentially activate lower trapezius (45-56% MVIC) and serratus anterior 
while minimizing upper trapezius dominance. Critically, unilateral hanging with 
body oscillation theoretically induces rotational perturbations that trigger 
feedforward activation of deep rotator cuff muscles (subscapularis: 37% MVIC; 
infraspinatus: 28% MVIC in perturbation studies), potentially addressing deep 
stabilizer dysfunction inaccessible through conventional exercise.
CONCLUSIONS: Brachiation-based exercise presents a theoretically coherent 
approach to UCS rehabilitation that addresses multiple muscle imbalances 
simultaneously through evolutionarily congruent movement patterns. Empirical 
validation through randomized controlled trials measuring EMG outcomes is 
warranted.
