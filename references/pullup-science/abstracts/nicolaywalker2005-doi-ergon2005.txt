WHAT FOLLOWS IS A SECONDARY, WEBSEARCH-DERIVED SUMMARY, NOT THE PAPER'S OWN ABSTRACT --
flagged as such per this project's sourcing rules. It is Claude's paraphrase of a search-
engine result snippet (Semantic Scholar's public listing), not a verbatim quote:

- 51 subjects, aged 18-33, three experiments: a single maximal grip repetition, a
  10-repetition protocol, and a 30-second static (sustained) maximal handgrip hold.
- Forearm/hand anthropometric measurements predicted absolute grip strength better than
  height or weight; predictive accuracy was highest for the single-repetition test and
  declined as protocol duration increased (i.e., strength is a weaker predictor of
  endurance than of peak force).
- Anthropometric variation was NOT associated with relative grip endurance (percent
  decline in force output over the protocol) -- bigger hands/forearms do not fatigue
  proportionally less.
- The dominant hand was significantly stronger than the non-dominant hand but ALSO fatigued
  MORE rapidly (a larger relative force decline), a trend more pronounced in women than men.

Relevance to this project: the 30-second static maximal hold is the closest published
protocol duration to a single hard set (Dennis's ~45 s of near-maximal effort); the direction
of the dominant-hand finding (stronger but fatigues faster) is consistent with a peripheral,
occlusion-driven fatigue mechanism (see Byström & Kilbom 1990, this directory) rather than a
central one, since a stronger muscle contracting at a similar or higher %MVC would occlude
its own blood flow more severely. No literal %-force-decline number from this paper could be
retrieved this session -- do not report a numeric grip-decline percentage attributed to this
citation; the only usable content is the qualitative summary above.
