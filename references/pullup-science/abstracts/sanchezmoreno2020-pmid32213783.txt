Effects of Velocity Loss During Body Mass Prone-Grip Pull-up Training on Strength and Endurance

doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003500. PMID 32213783. Closed access (no PMC copy found).

Fetched from PubMed E-utilities on 2026-09-08.

ABSTRACT:
Twenty-nine strength-trained men were randomly assigned to one of two 8-week (16-session) velocity-
based, body-mass, prone-grip pull-up training programs differing only in the within-set velocity-loss
(VL) threshold allowed before stopping the set: VL25 (25% velocity loss) or VL50 (50% velocity loss).
The VL25 group showed significantly greater improvements than VL50 in estimated 1RM, and in mean
propulsive/peak velocity attained against all common loads. Maximum repetitions to failure improved
similarly in both groups (no significant between-group difference). The authors conclude that "once a
25% velocity loss is achieved during PU training, further repetitions did not elicit additional gains"
and, for this bodyweight pulling exercise, may even blunt strength/velocity adaptations relative to
stopping the set earlier, even though completing more reps (to 50% loss) did not further improve
absolute endurance (max reps).

Notes for this project: direct evidence that a 25%-velocity-loss cutoff is the practical
"still-productive" ceiling for a bodyweight pull-up training set; going to 50% loss buys no extra
strength/velocity adaptation. Useful as a training-prescription anchor (not the same claim as an RIR
count per rep, but converts naturally: if 25% cumulative loss over a whole set is the "worth stopping"
line for a fresh strength-focused set, the "reserve" figure the pipeline reports for a max-effort set
should be interpreted against this VL25 landmark rather than an arbitrary 9%/rep slope).
