One Velocity Loss Threshold Does Not Fit All: Consideration of Sex, Training Status, History, and
Personality Traits When Monitoring and Controlling Fatigue During Resistance Training.

ABSTRACT:
PURPOSE: quantify variability in volume-of-work completed after reaching different velocity-loss (VL)
thresholds, and test effects of sex, training status/history and personality traits, in the back squat.
METHODS: 46 resistance-trained people (15 female, 31 male, 18-40 y) did a 1RM test then two
repetitions-to-failure tests 72 h apart at 70/80/90% 1RM with 10 min inter-set rest. RESULTS: agreement
in the amount of work completed across two testing sessions at the same nominal VL threshold was "not
acceptable" — i.e. a fixed VL% does not reproducibly correspond to a fixed number of reps done, even in
the same person on a different day. Regardless of load, females completed more reps than males at every
VL threshold while males lifted at higher absolute velocities; people with higher trait emotional
stability also completed more reps at a given VL threshold. Sex, load choice, strength/training history
and emotional stability all affected how linear the rep-velocity relationship was and therefore where a
set actually terminated.
CONCLUSION: using one universal VL threshold for everyone will produce inconsistent acute training
stimuli and, over time, divergent adaptations; sex, training status/history and psychological traits
should be considered when setting or interpreting VL thresholds.

Notes for this project: strong direct caution against the pipeline's current single fixed constant
(9% loss = 1 RIR for everyone). This is squat, not pull-up, and free-weight loaded not bodyweight, but
the general finding — same %VL does not mean the same number of reps left, and varies systematically by
sex/training history/psychological trait — should be stated as an explicit caveat wherever the new
pull-up RIR formula is presented, and argues for a personally-calibrated (first-rep or fastest-rep
referenced) model over a universal fixed threshold.
