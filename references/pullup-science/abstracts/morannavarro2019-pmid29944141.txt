ABSTRACT:
Thirty men of varying resistance-training levels performed sets to failure at 65%, 75% and 85% 1RM
across four different exercises (multijoint upper- and lower-body). The velocity at which a lifter
would need to stop a set to leave a given number of repetitions in reserve (2, 4, 6 or 8 RIR) was
highly consistent across loads and across the four exercises, and had high between-session reliability
(coefficient of variation 4.4-8.0%); training level did not meaningfully change these "stop velocities."
The authors conclude that the velocity at which N reps remain in reserve is a stable, generalisable
value (an "absolute velocity for X-RIR" table) that can be used instead of subjective RPE/RIR ratings
to control proximity to failure objectively.

Notes for this project: this is the general (non-pull-up) evidence that "velocity associated with N RIR"
is a more stable target than a linear %-loss-per-rep rule, and that this stop-velocity generalises
reasonably across exercises/loads within a person. Combined with Sánchez-Moreno 2017's pull-up-specific
V1RM (~0.20 m/s) and the pull-up RTF/velocity-loss curve, this supports building the new RIR formula
around a small lookup/regression of "velocity relative to this person's V1RM (or first-rep V0) -> RIR",
rather than the current constant-slope 9%-per-rep model, while flagging that Jukic et al. 2023 (above)
found the SAME kind of relationship is not perfectly universal (sex/training-history effects exist).
